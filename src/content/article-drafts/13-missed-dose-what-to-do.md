# Missed a Dose? What to Do Next

<p class="dr-speakable-answer">
If you miss a dose, the general rule printed on most medication labels is to take it as soon as you remember — unless it is almost time for the next dose, in which case skip the missed one and continue as normal. Do not take two doses to catch up. This rule has real exceptions (birth control, warfarin, insulin, some heart and seizure medicines have their own instructions), so check your leaflet or ask your pharmacist for the specific drug.
</p>

Missing a dose is normal. Roughly half of people on long-term medication do not take it exactly as prescribed, so the useful question is not "how do I never miss one" but "what do I do right now, and how do I stop this particular dose slipping again".

## The general rule, and why it exists

Most medicines are dosed to hold a steady level in your blood. Taking a late dose usually restores that level. Taking two doses close together can push it above the safe range, which is why "catching up" is the part that causes harm, not the missing.

So the standard guidance from the [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/medicines/) and [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/medicines.html) is:

1. **Remembered early?** Take it now, then go back to your normal schedule.
2. **Almost time for the next dose?** Skip the missed one. Take the next dose at its usual time.
3. **Never double up** unless the label or your prescriber explicitly says to.
4. **Unsure?** Call your pharmacy. This is a free, fast, no-appointment question and they answer it all day.

"Almost time" is not a fixed number — for a once-daily tablet it might mean within a few hours, for something taken every 6 hours it is much tighter. When in doubt, the leaflet in the box states it for your drug.

## Medications that do not follow the general rule

Some categories have their own missed-dose instructions, and guessing is a bad idea:

- **Hormonal birth control** — the correct action depends on the pill type and which week you are in; back-up contraception may be needed.
- **Warfarin and other anticoagulants** — dosing is tuned to blood test results; call the clinic that manages it.
- **Insulin and diabetes medication** — depends on the type, your food intake and your current glucose.
- **Seizure medication** — missed doses can trigger breakthrough seizures; there are specific catch-up instructions.
- **Antibiotics** — finish the full course; missed doses shift the schedule but do not shorten the course.
- **Weekly injections such as GLP-1s** — most have a defined window (often several days) to take a late dose before skipping to the next scheduled one.

For any of these, the instruction sheet that came with the prescription is the authority, and your pharmacist is the shortcut.

## What to do after you have handled it

Log the miss. This is the step most people skip, and it is the one that matters at your next appointment. A record showing "missed the evening dose four times this month, always on days I worked late" is a clinical fact your prescriber can act on — maybe the dose moves to morning, maybe the formulation changes. "I sometimes forget" is not actionable.

## Stopping the same dose from slipping again

Missed doses are rarely random. They cluster around one time of day, usually the one with no fixed anchor.

- **Anchor it to something you already do** — brushing teeth, the coffee machine, locking the front door. Time-based alarms fail when your day moves; habit anchors move with you.
- **Fix the notification, not your memory** — on iPhone, mark reminders time-sensitive so they break through Focus; on Android, exempt the app from battery optimization. Both are covered in [setting up medication reminders](/articles/set-up-medication-reminder-health-app).
- **Move the dose if the label allows** — an evening dose you miss weekly is worse than a morning dose you take daily. Ask whether the timing is flexible.
- **Use a second nudge for the hard one** — a follow-up reminder 30 minutes later catches the "I'll do it in a minute" miss.
- **Keep a spare where you actually are** — a small supply at work or in a bag covers the not-at-home miss, where storage instructions allow.

If missed doses are frequent rather than occasional, a proper reminder setup helps more than willpower. ## When to call someone instead of reading

Call your prescriber, pharmacist or a nurse line if:

- You took a double dose, or two doses close together, by mistake.
- You have missed several doses in a row of a medicine you were told not to stop suddenly.
- You have new symptoms after the missed dose.
- The medicine is on the list above and you cannot find the instructions.

If someone has taken far too much, contact emergency services or a poison control center immediately rather than waiting to see what happens.

## FAQs

**What should I do if I miss a dose of my medication?**
Take it as soon as you remember, unless it is nearly time for your next dose — then skip it and carry on as normal. Never take two doses to catch up. Some medicines, including birth control, warfarin, insulin and seizure medication, have their own instructions, so check the leaflet or ask your pharmacist.

**Is it safe to take two doses to catch up?**
Generally no. Doubling up can push the drug level above the safe range and is the main way a missed dose turns into a real problem. Only double up if the label or your prescriber specifically tells you to.

**How late is too late to take a missed dose?**
It depends on how often the medicine is taken. For a once-daily tablet, several hours late is usually fine; for something taken every few hours, the window is much shorter. The leaflet in the box gives the answer for your drug.

**Does missing one dose ruin my treatment?**
Usually not. A single missed dose of most long-term medicines is a blip. Repeated misses are what change how well treatment works — which is why logging them and spotting the pattern matters more than any one lapse.

**Should I tell my doctor about missed doses?**
Yes. Prescribers assume some doses are missed; knowing when and how often lets them adjust timing or dose instead of assuming the medicine is not working. Bringing a log to the appointment makes that conversation quick.
